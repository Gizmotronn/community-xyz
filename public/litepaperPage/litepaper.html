The problem. We currently fund an illness economy. Ageing populations and chronic disease have pushed systems toward late-stage rescue while prevention and self-care remain underpowered. Incentives for the 3 Ps - Payors, Providers, Patients - are misaligned, creating friction, waste, and uneven outcomes. 1. The Problem

Part I: Societies Flywheel of an Illness Economy

We’re funding sickness, not health. Around the world, systems have drifted into an illness economy: capital, workforce, and technology concentrate on late-stage rescue while prevention and self-care remain underpowered. As populations age, that imbalance becomes untenable. By 2050, the global 60+ population is projected to nearly double to ~2.1B, intensifying demand and exposing how poorly current models scale for chronic, long-duration care (UN Population). Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) already cause the majority of deaths and dominate spend, yet the operating model still reacts late, at high cost, with uneven outcomes (WHO Global NCDs; WEF Tackling Chronic Disease; AHRQ Multiple Chronic Conditions).

The “3 Ps” are misaligned. In practice, Payors (insurers/the state), Providers (clinics/hospitals), and Patients (people) often pull against one another. Payors optimise budgets and risk; providers optimise throughput and reimbursement rules; patients face daily constraints of time, literacy, stress, and access. The result is friction, waste, and variable quality, not a flywheel. In fixed-budget systems, cost pressure crowds out prevention; in fee-for-service systems, incentives tilt toward activity over activation. Either way, the 3 Ps operate in a zero-sum frame where one party “wins” only when another “pays,” leaving population health under-served.

Chronic conditions drive the bill. A small set of conditions consumes a disproportionate share of resources and human potential. Analyses attribute ~80% of total healthcare spending in high-income settings to chronic conditions; the prevalence of long-term conditions keeps rising (WEF report; AHRQ MCC; DH Long-Term Conditions (UK)). Quality of life declines, productivity falls, and families carry hidden caregiving and financial burdens (Megari QoL review). Meanwhile, eight everyday behaviours—tobacco, alcohol, inactivity, poor diet, non-adherence, poor stress management, insufficient sleep, and missed screening—drive much of the burden. Adhering to even a subset of healthy behaviours can dramatically reduce risk (e.g., ~93% lower risk of type 2 diabetes, ~81% lower MI risk)—yet most systems do not consistently activate people to make and sustain these changes (WEF; Arch Intern Med study).

Macroeconomics amplify the strain. Health already commands double-digit shares of GDP in many regions and has climbed sharply over two decades (e.g., UK spend more than tripled from 2000 to 2020) (ONS UK Health Accounts; EU Fiscal Sustainability). Without a durable shift from late-stage rescue to earlier activation, the curve is unstable. Policymakers acknowledge the need for more effective, accessible, resilient systems, but turning principle into practice has been slow without mechanisms that make prevention measurable, investable, and scalable (EU Commission report).

Fragmented stakeholders, missing structure. Beyond the 3 Ps, the ecosystem includes public health, policymakers, pharma, and med-tech—each with legitimate objectives but no shared coordination surface (Stakeholders in healthcare ecosystem; Kanter “PALs” collaboration). Collaboration is often ad hoc, data siloed, and “success” tied to short-term pilots that don’t compound. Without a common way to recognise and reward patient activation—the knowledge, skills, and confidence to self-manage—systems default to treating complications instead of building capability upstream (Alma-Ata Declaration; WHO Global NCDs; WHO Health 2020).

We’ve known the direction for decades—just not how to scale it. The Alma-Ata Declaration (1978) called for community participation and empowerment as a foundation of primary health care (WHO Alma-Ata). Early community-health worker models (e.g., “Barefoot Doctors”) showed what’s possible when knowledge and support live close to people’s lives. Today, the world is digitally connected but physically fractured. What’s missing is a way to turn authentic, local community cooperation into verifiable signals that stakeholders can trust and fund—so doing the right thing becomes the easiest thing for everyone involved (WHO Alma-Ata; Kanter collaboration; Forbes “Healthcare Flywheel”).

Part II: The Human Reality - Short-Term Choices in a World Without Belonging

Incoherent signals make the “quick hit” rational. When the 3 Ps pull apart, people encounter disjointed health messages—a clinic leaflet here, a public campaign there, a new app tomorrow, a viral thread that contradicts yesterday’s advice. Nothing compounds into a clear, trusted path. In that noise, the attention economy fills the gap: feeds optimised for engagement, not empowerment. Short-term gratification—fast calories, binge scrolls, skipped screenings, missed refills—becomes the predictable default. These are not moral failings; they are rational responses to conflicting incentives and weak social scaffolding.

Belonging is the missing infrastructure. Humans sustain hard behaviours when they belong to groups that make those behaviours normal, visible, and valued. Yet durable, supportive health communities—local enough to feel real, structured enough to last—are scarce. People crave belonging and often find it in tribes that rarely translate into healthier days. Without ongoing trusted peer voices (tacit knowledge) paired with crisp expert clarity (explicit knowledge), self-care is isolated willpower against systems designed for the opposite.

Fragmentation erodes trust. Conflicting advice from institutions, influencers, and vendors breeds scepticism (“who is this for?”). Patients experience care as transactional and episodic; providers feel rushed; payors communicate in policy and denials. The trust vacuum is filled by whatever feels immediate and certain—even when it’s unhelpful. Until we replace contradiction with coherent guidance and solitude with authentic community, we will keep asking individuals to win a daily willpower contest they are set up to lose.

From spreadsheets to street-level reality. The macro failures of the illness economy show up in micro-choices—the lunch skipped, the refill missed, the walk deferred, the screening ignored—because support, clarity, and belonging are missing at the moment of decision. Any credible fix must reconnect the societal engine (aligned incentives, measurable prevention) to the human engine (belonging that normalises healthier behaviour). Only then do short-term choices begin to bend toward long-term health.

That’s why Health-Shared starts where health actually happens: in communities. With 39,700+ registered members across 20+ early adopter communities, we already see the pattern: when lived experience is surfaced and paired with clear expert guidance, people show up more often—and stick with it.

2. Our Fix

Part I: Societal Solution - Align the system around verifiable community activation

If the illness economy is a system problem, the remedy must give the system something it can see, trust, and fund. Health-Shared + Health Protocol create that shared surface. Communities are stood up in partnership with real-world organisers (public health teams, providers, NGOs) and run human-led, AI-assisted so they feel local and authentic while orchestration happens under the hood. Each community begins with organiser inputs (priority cohorts, languages, safeguards, incentive rules) and a short, incentivised discovery round with local “ambassadors.” The platform’s thematic analysis turns these inputs into a clear map of what people need and what actually helped, then pairs those themes with expert guidance and schedules them into a weekly feed that fits culture and context.

Participation flows privacy-safely into anonymised Health Scorecards—public, verifiable “receipts of activation” that show which communities are building capability over time (not names, never PHI). With that evidence in the open, payors, public health, research sponsors, employers, and NGOs can choose when to support communities that perform—moving from promises to proof. Funding strengthens incentives → incentives drive activation → activation improves scorecards—finally giving the 3 Ps a positive-sum flywheel to align around prevention and self-care. This is exactly the kind of measurable, investable prevention policymakers have been calling for in the WHO health-promotion traditionand regional strategies like Health 2020 (WHO Health Promotion glossary; WHO Health 2020). It complements mainstream system aims to raise activation and self-management capacity (WHO self-care guideline; NHS Long Term Plan).

Crucially, the approach is governance-ready: communities can operate as DAOs or under local authorities; value flows are lawful and auditable; and points are proof of contribution, not money—ensuring incentives remain pro-social, measurable, and aligned with outcomes. Over time, this shared, verifiable coordination layer lets systems route budget upstream with confidence—turning prevention from a nice idea into a trackable, fundable reality. Evidence suggests that when people are activated, outcomes and experiences improve (Hibbard & Greene), adherence strengthens when information access is practical (OpenNotes adherence study), and communities of practice raise performance by improving knowledge flow (Wenger et al.; IBM CoP & performance; BMC CoP systematic review).

Part II: For Individuals - Belonging & Authentic Community

People don’t change because they’re lectured; they change when they belong. Health-Shared is built to make that belonging feel real—with technology assisting, not replacing, the human centre. We pair two fuels of learning:

  • Light (explicit knowledge): concise, trustworthy guidance from clinicians and experts.
  • Warmth (tacit knowledge): lived-experience stories—hopes, fears, solutions, strategies, successes, and failures—that you can’t Google.

From the first ambassador interviews, our continuous, iterative content co-creation loop kicks in. Interviews are edited into bite-sized, consent-cleared stories (warmth). Each theme is paired with crisp expert guidance (light). A scheduler sequences light + warmth into a weekly, culturally tuned feed. Then the community itself fine-tunes it: saves, replies, and “helpful” marks signal what resonates; the system elevates what works, trims what doesn’t, and proposes new prompts—week after week. In knowledge-management terms, this blends explicit and tacit knowledge into shared “mindlines”people can actually use (Prusak, Knowledge in Organisations; Polanyi, Personal Knowledge; Gabbay & le May, Mindlines; Edgar & Albright, KM review).

We then recognise community-elevating actions with points (proof, not cash): checking in regularly, sharing what worked, inviting peers, answering quick discovery interviews, helping moderate, liking/sharing useful posts, and (optionally) linking health data privately to enrich one’s scorecard. These light-touch incentives reward the behaviours that lift others, not just individual grind. Research supports that peer communities and modest incentives can strengthen adherence and self-care—whether in walking programmes or glucose control—when they’re embedded in real support structures (Richardson et al.; Ann Intern Med peer-mentoring). And the design aligns with behavioural science: capability, opportunity, and motivation (COM-B) are addressed together—knowledge and tools (capability), a social place to practise (opportunity), and recognition that sustains effort (motivation) (Michie et al.; McKinsey report on behaviour change).

Privacy stays by default: individuals control their data; only anonymised, aggregated signals flow to scorecards. For members, the payoff is belonging that normalises healthier choices. For the ecosystem, those choices become verifiable signals that can be funded responsibly. In disadvantaged settings especially, structured community engagement has been linked to improved outcomes and reduced isolation—precisely what this design amplifies at scale (Cyril et al.; Social prescribing review; PLOS ONE CoP review).

Bottom line: belonging lights the fire; continuous co-creation keeps it fed; points make contribution visible; scorecards make progress verifiable. The result is a human engine that bends daily choices toward long-term health—while giving systems the receipts they need to back what works.

4. Health-Shared, Health Protocol, and the Nested Game Theory Driving Scale

Health Protocol is designed to hyper-scale health-shared communities through the Web3 principles of decentralisation, alignment of donor stakeholders with communities and providing rails for an auditable transfer for value.

However, Web3, success isn’t just about technology — it’s about human behaviour. The ecosystems that thrive are those built around game theory: the study of how people make strategic decisions, and how incentives can align to drive cooperation, growth, and sustainability.

Health-Shared and Health Protocol represents the implementation of a nested, multi-level game structure that rewards both community members and investors when they act early and align their interests to communities relevant to them.

Two simple but powerful coordination memes define the system:

(3,3) — the inner game, where community members choose whether to cooperate early and activate their health community.
(5,5) — the outer game, where investors, stakers, healthcare organisations, and donors coordinate to fund, scale, and grow the ecosystem.

These games aren’t isolated. They are stacked, meaning success in the community activation game (3,3) directly improves the dynamics and payoffs of the investor and stakeholder game (5,5).

Together, they create a scalable system where communities, healthcare organisations, public health, pharmacy chains, and investors all win — if they play the game together.

The Inner Game (3,3): Why Authentic Communities Win Through Cooperation

Inside Health-shared.com , we’ve designed community activation as a cooperative game. Community members earn game points and real-world incentives for actions that grow the community and improve health outcomes:

  • Logging in regularly
  • Participating in discovery interviews
  • Voting in polls
  • Tagging, liking, or sharing content
  • Answering AI-generated questions
  • Referring new members
  • Linking health records and consenting to considering research opportunities

But beyond micro-incentives, the structure of the game itself motivates early, cooperative action. Important in this structure is the magnitude of rewards - these are linked to supply and demand of the rewardable action being incentivised. Early on, the supply is low and hence the rewards are higher.

Stacked Games: How the Inner and Outer Games Reinforce Each Other

As previously alluded to, this is an example of a nested, multi-level game, where the success of one layer conditions the payoffs of the other:

  • Communities grow fastest when individuals play the (3,3) game well.
  • Strong communities attract donor funding and attention.
  • Donor funding and Health Protocol fee-sharing kickstart the (5,5) game.
  • Early investors, stakers, and partners benefit most when they support communities early.
  • Treasury growth, incentives, and ecosystem expansion flow back to support communities.

The result is a self-reinforcing flywheel of activation, funding, and shared success — driven by game theory, designed for scale.

5. Health Scorecards: The Data Layer Bridging (3,3) → (5,5)

Health Scorecards are the bridge between cooperation and capital. They are privacy-preserving, anonymised receipts of activation—public enough for accountability, private enough for trust. Layer 1 captures community activation: aggregated signals such as weekly check-ins, helpful shares, invitations accepted, Q&A participation, interview contributions, and moderation touchpoints. Layer 2 (opt-in) adds EHR-derived summaries: an AI agent, with explicit permission and partner approval, extracts privacy-safe endpoints off-chain; only anonymised aggregates reach the scorecard. Layer 3 (opt-in) can include consumer genomics summaries (e.g., 23andMe) at a community level. At no point is PHI placed on-chain. The result is a verifiable picture of momentum that lets funders route support responsibly.

7. Economic Flows & Fee Splits: Turning Proof into Support

Funding enters the system in three ways: partner funding (organisations funding their own communities), a Community Reserve (a protocol pool that advances incubation funds to new communities), and donor stakeholders (public health, systems, insurers, NGOs, employers, pharma, brands). Inbound donor value is programmatically split across (1) participant incentives (if the donor funds a specific community action), (2) the local Community DAO reserve(sustaining incentives and ops), (3) an onboarding facilitator (optional—if an organisation helped stand up the community), and (4) the Health Protocol treasury (safety, PoL, grants). To keep momentum visible and “alive,” distributions are made on a daily (or per-day-of-epoch) cadence, with on-chain receipts. Communities that subscribe to managed services (via Health-Shared) can qualify for preferential splits, accelerating maturation and safeguards.

Incentive Pricing & Community Phases: From Incubation to Income

Every community grows through two broad stages, and incentives should reflect that reality.

Incubation phase: The goal is to grow the core, surface insights, and build a sense of belonging. Incentives are priced within rails (min–max) for high-leverage actions: discovery interviews, inviting peers, Q&A participation, onboarding, and (where appropriate) linking EHR. Prices adjust by supply/demand—if interviews lag, their reward nudges up. In the

Income-generating phase: Donor programs begin funding community-positive actions tied to their objectives (e.g., peri-op preparation bundles, adherence check-ins, research pre-screening). As donor inflows rise, incubation supports taper and the community becomes self-propelling. The result is an economic on-ramp that rewards early effort while sustaining long-term usefulness.

8. Token & Value Accrual (HLTH)

HLTH is the protocol’s utility token. Its role is to coordinate routing, staking and ecosystem incentives. A minimal routing fee applies to donor value that traverses the rails.

We separate concerns: community points ≠ HLTH.

Points are proof of contribution inside communities; HLTH is the protocol utility that keeps rails fair, visible, and reliable.

Value accrues as more donors route more programs and as new use cases (trials, employer wellness, insurers, NGOs) adopt the scorecard standard.

  • HLTH holders vote on bounded parameters: fee caps, APY rails, reporting cadence, and Community Reserve allocations.
  • Critical controls (pauses, emergency rails) require multi-sig + time-lock + quorum.

8.2 Multi-Pool Liquidity & Meta-Router (How Flow Meets Depth)

Open pool model.
Qualified operators can deploy HLTH↔X pools (e.g., HLTH/USDC, HLTH/ETH). Pools compete on depth, fee, and reliability.

Meta-router.
For each swap/settlement, the router computes landed cost per path:

  • Marginal price impact under CPMM at the requested quote size (depth at ±1% bands)
  • Pool fee (bps) + venue/gas overhead
  • Reliability score (oracle alignment, uptime, slippage variance)

The router executes against the cheapest eligible route. Operators who deepen liquidity and optimise fees win more flow and earn more LP fees. Competition compresses spreads for donors and stabilises execution for communities.

8.3 Value Accrual Mechanics

More programs → more routing.
Each donor program (public health, insurer, employer, NGO, pharma) that funds communities via Health Protocol generates routing fees and usage fees. Fees are settled in HLTH (directly or via auto-swap).

More scorecards → more usage.
As scorecards become a standard signal for “verified activation,” Explorer traffic grows. These usage fees accrue to the protocol in HLTH.

Cheaper execution → larger fee pie.
The meta-router’s cost discipline + pool competition minimise slippage. Lower execution costs leave more net fees to recycle into the flywheel (Community Reserve, POL, grants).

Staking demand.
Operators stake HLTH for yield and router tie-breaks, creating locking demand tied to real network use rather than pure speculation.

Reflexive loop.
Donor volume ↑ → fees ↑ → Community Reserve ↑ (more incentives) → activation ↑ → scorecards ↑ → donor confidence ↑ → volume ↑. HLTH captures this via fee settlement and staking demand.

8.4 Protocol-Owned Liquidity (POL) Strategy

Purpose.
POL ensures baseline depth for donor settlements, reduces reliance on mercenary liquidity, and captures LP fees back to Treasury.

Allocation.

  • Core coverage: concentrate POL in at least one blue-chip HLTH↔stable pair to guarantee minimum depth and predictable price impact.
  • Tactical overlays: deploy incremental POL to third-party pools that consistently win router flow on cost + reliability, reinforcing efficient venues.

Sourcing & recycling.
A fixed share of routing/usage fees auto-accrues to the Treasury. Treasury periodically converts a portion to POL per policy (on-chain), subject to risk rails (max % treasury per pool, min time-weighted depth, epoch rebalancing). POL fee income then recycles to:

  • Top up Community Reserve (sustained incentives)
  • Maintain/expand POL (execution quality)
  • Fund operations and grants (safety & ecosystem)

Transparency.
Each epoch publishes POL deltas, pool distribution, fee capture, and reserve movements.

8.5 Fee Flow & Recycling (Sustaining Incentives)

Inbound fees (in HLTH or auto-swapped):

  • Community Reserve — funds community-elevating actions (check-ins, shared learnings, invites, interviews, moderation; points are non-transferable).
  • Treasury → POL — deepens efficient pools; lowers landed cost; captures LP fees.
  • Ops/Safety — audits, infra, compliance.
  • Builder Grants — Explorer indices, standards, integrations.

Outcome.
As routing and usage rise, more HLTH fees are recycled to the Reserve and POL. Incentives remain funded without unstable emissions; execution stays cheap; growth compounds.

9. Staking & Liquidity: A Competitive Multi-Pool With a Smart Meta-Router

Health Protocol’s liquidity is designed to keep donor value moving cheaply and reliably so communities never miss a beat. To do that at scale, we run a competitive multi-pool model rather than a single, monolithic pool. Any qualified organisation can stand up its own HLTH↔X pool (HLTH/USDC, HLTH/ETH, HLTH/RWA-stable, etc.). A meta-router then scans every eligible venue on each trade and sources the lowest landed cost path in real time.

In practice, “cheapest” isn’t just headline price. The router evaluates marginal price impact under CPMM (depth at the requested quote size), pool fees, gas/venue overheads, and a reliability score (oracle alignment, downtime, slippage variance). The route with the best all-in cost wins the order. This creates an incentive-aligned race: operators who deepen their pools and tune fees attract more protocol flow and earn more LP fees. Competition drives spreads down for donors and keeps execution predictable for communities.

Epoch Economics, Not Emissions Games

We still pay for liquidity, but only for what we need and when we need it. Rewards run on epochs (e.g., weekly/bi-weekly) with conservative APY rails set by governance, so yields can’t whipsaw. Inside those rails, each pool’s epoch APY adjusts automatically from three transparent signals measured last epoch:

  • Utilisation / landed-cost wins: pools that actually cleared more protocol flow at competitive cost get rewarded; idle TVL doesn’t.
  • Depth vs. target bands: if a pool sits below target depth (e.g., value available at ±1% price), its APY nudges up to recruit liquidity; if it’s over-provisioned, APY eases.
  • Stability & quality: better oracle alignment, uptime, and low slippage variance earn steadier emissions.

Net effect: emissions tilt toward useful, used, reliable liquidity, not just the biggest headline TVL. For operators, the levers are clear—add depth, tighten fees (within bounds), and keep your venue healthy. For the protocol, this keeps the “cost of liquidity” proportional to the work it does for communities.

POL as the Shock Absorber

Alongside competitive pools, the protocol grows Protocol-Owned Liquidity (POL) from a fixed share of routing and usage fees. POL concentrates in one or two blue-chip HLTH↔stable pairs to guarantee baseline depth, then adds tactical overlays to third-party pools that routinely win the router on cost and reliability. This does three things at once: reduces donor slippage, lowers dependence on mercenary liquidity, and recaptures LP fees back into the treasury. Deployment follows strict safety rails (max treasury at risk per pool, minimum time-weighted depth, periodic rebalancing) and is reported on-chain each epoch.

Fee Recycling Keeps the Flywheel Turning

Every routed dollar tells the system what to fund next. A governance-bounded split of routing/usage fees tops up the Community Reserve (fueling community-elevating incentives), deepens POL (stability), and covers ops and grants(safety + new indices and Explorer features). Because the meta-router routes to the cheapest liquidity, more of each fee dollar can be recycled back into incentives—driving the core loop: actions → proof → support → more actions.

Controls, Transparency, and a Straightforward UI

Risk is managed with epoch caps on emissions, max APY step-changes, venue whitelists, TWAP/oracle checks, per-pool pauses if needed, and disclosure you can underwrite. Each epoch ships a public report: donor volume, average landed cost, per-pool utilisation, emissions paid, POL delta, and Community Reserve top-ups.

On the front-end, think Olympus-style clarity adapted to our rails: wallet connect, TVL, POL share, depth at ±1% price, 30-day volume, current APY and next-epoch band, plus a Pool Leaderboard that surfaces which pools the router actually used and why. Stakers get stake/unstake (with a short cooldown), claim, and auto-compound. Operators get the signals they need to compete. Donors get receipts that their value traversed the lowest-cost, highest-reliability path.

Bottom line: a market that competes to serve communities. Liquidity providers deepen pools to win flow. The router relentlessly picks the cheapest route. Fees recycle into incentives and POL. Communities stay funded. Donor value lands where it works.

10. Data & Privacy Architecture: PHI-Safe by Design

Health Protocol separates what must be public from what must stay private. Non-PHI data: Activation points etc go on-chain to power the Explorer and donor alignment. PHI remains in secure buckets on the Health-Shared backend under purpose-limited agreements. Members permission AI agents to perform specific tasks (e.g., health conditions extraction for scorecard endpoints, trial pre-screening). These agents run off-chain, produce anonymised summaries or eligibility flags, and write only privacy-safe aggregates on-chain.

Everything is audited: who ran what, for whom, and under which consent. This architecture allows communities to prove momentum while respecting the most important constraint of all: People’s trust.

12. Governance, Compliance & Safeguards

Governance, Compliance & Safeguards

Our promise is simple: no PHI on-chain. Scorecards publish only anonymised, community-level metrics with optional privacy-preserving constraints (e.g., minimum cohort sizes). Programs route value to Community DAOs (with oversight) or local authorities, always under local law, with DPIA/DSA templates and purpose-limited use. Communities support self-care and navigation; they do not diagnose. Moderation playbooks, escalation routes, and audit trails are non-negotiable. Governance follows progressive decentralisation: a foundation stewards standards and the attesters registry; governance-lite opens parameter setting; and partner councils/delegates steer classes of programs (e.g., public health, trials, workplace).
